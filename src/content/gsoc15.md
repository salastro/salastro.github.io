This is the last week of coding, and it landed on the piece of work that everything else this summer had been quietly building toward: turning `manualintegrate`'s tangle of self-recursive rules into a single `IntegrationSolver` that owns the recursion on their behalf.

Under the old design, every rule that needed a subproblem solved just called back into the integration machinery directly, recursively, wherever it happened to sit in the code. That works fine until you want something that cuts across all of those call sites at once, like a depth limit, a memoization cache, or a switch to turn branching on or off. You either thread that state through every rule by hand, or you give up and let each rule reinvent its own version of it. The fix this week was to stop letting rules recurse at all. A rule function now either returns a `Rule` directly, or is a generator that `yield`s an `IntegralInfo` whenever it hits a subproblem, and the solver sends the resulting `Rule` back in. The rule never touches the recursion itself; it just asks for an answer and waits. That single inversion is what makes `max_depth`, `branch`, and the memo cache possible as one shared piece of state instead of scattered bookkeeping repeated in a dozen places.

The best bug of the week came directly out of that change, and it was the kind of bug that makes you distrust your own numbers. `test_issue_7130` finished in 16.5 seconds with `max_depth` set to 6, but setting the depth to 100, which should only ever do more work in pathological cases, took unreasonably long. The instinct is to assume a deeper budget is doing something expensive on purpose. What was actually happening was that memoization only ran when `max_depth is None`, so setting *any* integer cap silently turned the cache off entirely, regardless of how generous the cap was. Depth and caching had been coupled for no real reason. The fix was to stop gating the cache on the depth budget and gate it instead on whether a result is "starved," meaning it still contains a `DontKnowRule` somewhere inside it. A starved result depends on how much budget was left when it was computed and can't be trusted at a different depth, but a clean result is clean no matter what budget produced it, so it's always safe to cache. It's a small distinction, but conflating "how much work we were allowed to do" with "whether the answer is trustworthy" is exactly the kind of thing that's invisible until a benchmark makes it obvious.

Something I hadn't planned for was a second, independent rewrite of the same migration landing at almost the same time, from a different contributor. Comparing the two diffs in review turned up two bugs neither of us had caught alone: a `PolynomialError` on radicands with a symbolic coefficient, and a runaway integration-by-parts loop on $x^2\sin(x^2+x)\cos(x^2+x)$. Both got fixed the same day. In return, I picked up their idea of writing the Bioche substitution as a nested generator closure instead of the 17-argument helper function it used to be, which is a much easier shape to read once you're used to the generator style. It's a good reminder that having two people attack the same refactor isn't wasted duplication if you actually diff the results against each other instead of just picking one.

The new architecture isn't a free win, though. Benchmarks on special-function integration sped up by roughly 30x, but the orthogonal-polynomial benchmarks got measurably slower from the overhead of running everything through the generator-driven solver instead of a bare function call. And there's a design question with a reviewer that's still open: whether the memo lookup should happen before or after the depth check, and whether `max_depth` should track Python's own recursion limit instead of being an arbitrary number. Neither answer is obvious, and I'd rather leave it unresolved than force a decision just to close the PR before the deadline.

The two issues I opened alongside the migration are really about what the new architecture makes possible rather than problems in isolation. The current post-processing step in `manualintegrate`, things like reordering `Piecewise` branches or factoring out `erf`/trig terms, is a hard-coded tail that runs regardless of which rule actually produced the result, a leftover from a time when the solver had no way to know which rule fired. With generators, a rule can yield a small `RuleFlag` that the solver records and the caller can check afterward, which turns "always run this cleanup and hope it's a no-op" into "run this cleanup exactly when it's warranted." The other issue, on removing the `factor()` call from `integrate()`, turned out to have a real counterexample: without it, a particular integral of $12\sqrt{\sin^2(x)+\cos^2(x)}\,|\sin(x)|\,|\cos(x)|$ never collapses to the $3/2$ it should, because factoring is what exposes the identity in the first place. Not every simplification is dead weight, even when it looks like one from a distance.

There's also a smaller, older observation that resurfaced this week almost as an aside: dropping `trigintegrate` in favor of the new rule set breaks four tests, and three of those are just cosmetically different but equivalent antiderivatives. The fourth is a genuinely different closed form coming from heuristic Risch instead of the manual rules. I've run into this exact shape of problem before, where "more correct" and "same as before" quietly stop meaning the same thing, and it's still not something I have a clean answer for. It feels like the right note to end the summer on: the migration this week gives `manualintegrate` a place to actually put answers to questions like that, even though most of them are still open.
